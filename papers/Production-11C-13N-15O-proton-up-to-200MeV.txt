Production cross sections of light positron emitters 11 C (t1/2 =20.36 min), 13 N (t1/2 =9.97 min)
and 15 O (t1/2 =122 s) have been measured for proton-induced reactions on the main relevant
constituents of the human body (C, N and O) from threshold up to 200 MeV of proton incident
energy. This has been accomplished by means of two complementary experiments at the clinical
proton treatment centers WPE (Essen) and HIT (Heidelberg), both in Germany. In the ﬁrst case,
the multi-foil activation technique was combined with PET imaging, a methodology previously
developed at CNA (Seville, Spain). In the second experiment, aimed at energies below 50 MeV, the
activation of single foils was measured with conventional LaBr3 detectors. In both cases the IAEA
monitor reaction nat Cu(p,x)63 Zn data was used for validation purposes.
The most complete and accurate set of excitation functions for the production of light-positron
emitters 11 C, 13 N and 15 O in proton induced reactions on human tissue up to 200 MeV is presented.
These data are needed for nuclear reaction model development and are extremely important to
improve the accuracy of the PET range veriﬁcation in proton radiotherapy. Seven excitation functions
have been measured: 12 C(p,x)11 C, 14 N(p,x)11 C, 14 N(p,x)13 N, 14 N(p,γ)15 O, 16 O(p,x)11 C, 16 O(p,x)13 N
and 16 O(p,x)15 O up to 200 MeV of proton incident energy.

INTRODUCTION AND MOTIVATION

Excitation function of light-positron emitters in protoninduced nuclear reactions are of fundamental interest for
in-vivo treatment veriﬁcation methods in proton radiotherapy. Additionally, the production cross sections are
very sensitive to reaction modeling mechanism and associated parameters and measured data can be used to
validate existing evaluated nuclear databases, or to improve nuclear reaction models in the energy range up to
200 MeV, especially in the range above 20 MeV where
existing data are scarce.
In the context of proton therapy, the Positron Emission
Tomography (PET) range veriﬁcation technique relies on
the comparison of the measured and estimated activity

distributions of β + emitters produced by the proton beam
in the patient, hence relying on the knowledge of the
reaction cross sections involved. In order to improve the
accuracy with which they are known, we have measured
the reaction cross sections that result in 11 C (t1/2 =20.36
min), 13 N (t1/2 =9.97 min) and 15 O (t1/2 =122 s) in the
main elements of the human body (C, N and O) in the
full energy range of interest, i.e., up to clinical energies of
200 MeV.
The main advantage of proton therapy over conventional radiation therapy is that it oﬀers a better conformation of the dose in the tumor volume while reducing
the dose delivered to the surrounding healthy tissues [1].
This is possible due to the particular way in which ions interact with the matter, showing a ﬁnite penetration depth
(range) at the end of which the dose deposition is maximum, resulting in what is known as the Bragg peak. The
mentioned sparing of the healthy tissue makes proton therapy highly recommended for tumors close to organs at risk
and in pediatric cases, as it reduces the long-term eﬀects
of the irradiation such as the appearance of secondary
tumours [2].
In order to fully exploit the beneﬁts of proton therapy it
is necessary to make sure that the dose is delivered according to the treatment planning. This can be accomplished
with a PET scanner by acquiring the activity map of the
patient corresponding to the β + decay of the radioactive
nuclei produced along the trace of the proton beam [3–5].
The PET image can be acquired during the treatment
(in-beam) or after the treatment (oﬄine), detecting the
decay of short- or long-lived β + emitters, respectively.
The PET measured activity proﬁles need then to be
compared with a calculation, usually made by Monte
Carlo simulations, in order to asses if the delivery of the
ﬁelds has been made according to the treatment planning. Leaving aside biological wash-out eﬀects [6], the
accuracy of the simulated activity distribution depends
on the underlying cross sections to produce the β + emitters of interest. In the case of delayed (oﬄine or in-room)
PET veriﬁcation, the isotopes of interest are 11 C, 13 N
and 15 O. At the clinical energies, the main reactions producing these isotopes are 12 C(p,x)11 C, 14 N(p,x)13 N and
16
O(p,x)15 O. Other reactions of interest are 16 O(p,x)11 C,
16
O(p,x)13 N, 14 N(p,x)11 C and 14 N(p,γ)15 O (see Table I
for details).
A limitation is that experimental data corresponding
to these reactions is quite limited, especially for energies
above 20 MeV, and shows sizable discrepancies [7, 8].
Therefore, new measurements and subsequent data evaluations are required in the full energy range of interest
in order to reduce uncertainties in the estimation of the
activity depth proﬁles, enabling then the aimed submillimetric beam range veriﬁcation [9, 10]. Such data needs
were highlighted at the IAEA Technical meeting held in
Vienna on December 2018 [11].

mentioned longer lived β + emitters have been measured

PET/CT scanner at WPE

The induced activity of all the irradiated foils (see Section III A) was determined through a single measurement
with a Siemens Healthineers - Biograph Vision PET/CT
scanner from the Nuclear Medicine department of the University Hospital Essen, located at the same building than
the WPE. This scanner is made of 3.2x3.2x20 mm3 LSO
crystals and silicon photomultiplier (SiPMs) providing a
time resolution of just 214 ps, allowing for time-of-ﬂight
position reconstruction that improves both contrast and
signal-to-noise ratio [17].
The PET/CT scanner is located close to the treatment
rooms, which allowed to transport the irradiated foils to
the PET scanner rapidly enough to measure the decay of
15
O, with a half-life of just 122 s.
The reliability of the PET scanner was assessed with
two diﬀerent experiments:
a) A calibrated 1.70(3) kBq 22 Na source was placed at
5 diﬀerent positions and PET images were taken in
intervals of 1 and 15 minutes. The short exposures
would result in values in agreement within 3.3%, a
reproducibility that improved down to 0.7% for the 15
minutes exposures.

Beam delivery system at WPE

The West German Proton Therapy Centre (WPE) is
a cyclotron-based proton therapy center with four treatments rooms (IBA ProteusPlus) located in Essen, Germany. The isochronous cyclotron accelerates protons to
an energy of 228 MeV, which can then be degraded to
a minimum of 100 MeV with a system consisting in a
wheel-mounted wedge followed by analyzing magnets and
energy slits [14]. The experiments were performed in the
ﬁxed beam treatment room, where the pencil beam scanning (PBS) mode was chosen in order to deliver the quasi
mono-energetic proton beams with less secondary particles compared to the passive beam scanning techniques.
The beam is deﬂected by dipole magnets in order to select
the desired lateral position on a time scale in the order of
milliseconds. A ﬁrst ionization chamber is placed at the
nozzle entrance checking the centering of the beam, then
two monitor chambers are placed close to the nozzle exit
to check the spot position and width. Table II summarizes
the nominal beam size (sigma for both x- and y-axis) at
the isocenter for each energy. The lateral spreading of the
beam as it traverses the degraders and target foils has
been checked using radiochromic foils.

LaBr3 (Ce) detectors used at HIT

The activity induced in the irradiated foils at HIT was
measured with a conventional γ-ray detection system consisting in two cylindrical 1.5”x1.5” LaBr3 (Ce) detectors.
These were installed in a room next to the irradiation
room, so the time span between the end of the irradiation
and the start of the measurement is minimized and allows
the measurement of the shortest-lived isotope (15 O).
The detection eﬃciency at a speciﬁc target-detector distance was determined with an accuracy of 1.4% from a
Geant4 [23] Monte Carlo simulation of the β + decay, validated experimentally with calibrated 137 Cs and 22 Na γray sources. This work led to conclude that the LaBr3 (Ce)
crystal size is actually 1.44”x1.44”, 12% smaller in volume
than indicated by the manufacturer. Ref. [12] provides a
detailed explanation of the validation of the corresponding
detection and β + conversion eﬃciencies.

METHODOLOGY

Multi-foil activation and PET/CT measurement
at WPE

The employed technique consists in the irradiation of a
set of stacks of thin foils made of diﬀerent materials which
activity in then determined in a single PET measurement
for all the foils. A detailed explanation and the validation
of this technique is given in Ref. [12].
In this work, three stacks (see Fig. 1) made of 10 thin
target foils rich in carbon (polyethylene, PE), oxygen (silicon dioxide, SiO2 ) and nitrogen (aluminium nitride, AlN)
have been irradiated at four diﬀerent energies, allowing
the measurement of the reaction cross sections of interest
from ∼30 MeV up to 200 MeV in small energy steps.
The target thicknesses were 1 mm for the PE and SiO2
foils and 0.64 mm in the case of AlN (see Table IV for
details). For the irradiations the thin foils were embedded
between 5 or 10 mm thick PE slabs (see Fig. 1, bottom)
in order to degrade the proton beam energy and to have a
beam of the desired energy in each foil. As the minimum
extracted energy at WPE is 100 MeV, a PE slab of 40 mm
was used in front of each stack to degrade the beam energy below 60 MeV. The optimization of the degraders
conﬁguration was assessed with Geant4 simulations, and
the thicknesses of the PE slabs used in each irradiation

The PBS delivery mode allows the irradiation of three

SiO2 , AlN, the monitor foils and Cu foils were inserted

the positrons into two 511 keV photons. This is necessary
because the range of the positrons emitted by 11 C, 13 N
and 15 O is of a few meters in air, and only 5, 6 and 9 mm,
respectively, in PE. Fig. 3 shows: (top) the disposition of
the irradiated foils in two layers within the PE matrix,
(bottom) the complete PE matrix inside the PET scanner.

within always less than 1%. The images obtained include

In the following, the results of this work for the production of 11 C, 13 N and 15 O on C, N and O are presented and discussed separately, comparing in each case
our results with those available in the literature, both the
experimental and evaluated data. Note that the ICRU63
library [27] is the extension to 250 MeV of ENDF/B-VII.0
(LA-150) library for several isotopes of particular interest in proton therapy (as the ones studied in this work),
whereas TENDL-2019 includes ENDF/B-VII.0 data whenever they are available, which is also the situation in the
present study. Therefore in the following we will refer to
all of them as the ENDF/B-VII.0 library.

Production of 11 C in C, O and N

There are quite some data sets in the literature for the
C(p,pn)11 C reaction, as it dominates the production of
11

therapy, but none of them until now covers the full energy

range of interest. From the data displayed in Fig. 8 one can
observe two main groups: one including the data of Hintz
et al. (1952) [28], Aamodt et al. (1952) [29], Bäumer et al.
(2019) [16] and Bäcker et al. (2019) [30], and another one

indicate an underestimation of this cross section in the
ENDF/B-VII.0 library.
This situation illustrates the need of new data with
enhance accuracy covering the full range of interest, as
done in this work.
The cross section reported herein is in very good agreement with Hintz et al. (1952) [28] and Aamodt et al. (1952)
[29], and also with the recent work by Bäumer et al. (2019)
[16] and Bäcker et al. (2019) [30], i.e. with the ﬁrst group
of measurement mentioned in the previous paragraph.

Production of 15 O in O and N

In delayed PET range veriﬁcation, the biological
washout of the generated isotopes plays an important role
in the feasibility of the technique. Isotopes with shorter
half-lifes are recommended to avoid this eﬀect. For instance, reducing the time span between irradiation and
measurement to detect 15 O, which is produced mainly by
the 16 O(p,x)15 O reaction (see Fig. 7, bottom), would reduce wash-out eﬀects. The increase in the interest in using
this isotope brings about new measurements, such as in
Horst et at. (2019) [33], Masuda et al. (2018) [38] and Akagi et al. (2013) [37], to complete the previous ones from
Sajjad et al. (1985) [44], Valentin et al. (1965) [41] and
Albouy et al. (1962) [35]. It seems that in this case, see
Fig. 10, our data and most previous measurements are in
good agreement, especially below ∼80 MeV, while above
that energy our data are slightly lower than all others. It is
589

noticeable that the shape of the ENDF/B-VII.0 library is
quite far from all experimental data, a diﬀerence that has
considerable eﬀects in the activation proﬁles estimations
necessary for PET range veriﬁcation.
Last, although its role in this context is very limited,
for the sake of completeness, the production of 15 O in
nitrogen has been obtained as well. In this particular
case, there is no previous measurement in the literature,
hence the importance of the new data set obtained in this
work. The evaluated data are not available in ENDF/BVII.0 library (and therefore neither they are in ICRU63
and TENDL-2019). The only data available are in older
TENDL libraries, calculated with TALYS code, which oﬀer
in general less conﬁdence.

V.

SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

In the last few years, there is an increased interest in
the proton therapy community to improve the dose delivery by means of PET range veriﬁcation [45, 46]. The
reliable implementation of this technique requires accurate production cross section data, speciﬁcally for the
the long-lived positron emitters 11 C, 13 N and 15 O in the

Production of 11 C, 13 N and 15 O. . .

NUCLEAR DATA SHEETS

energy range up to 200 MeV. In this context, new measurements with minimized uncertainties should lead to
higher accuracy in the cross sections used in the Monte
Carlo calculations which determine the β + activity distributions in the body needed for PET range veriﬁcation
[8]. Furthermore, these data are also extremely useful for
the development of more precise reaction models and to
improve existing evaluated databases.
The experimental method employed in this work combines the common multi-foil activation technique with
the use of a PET scanner as detector. This allows measuring the activity of all the foils at the same time in a
single PET acquisition, hence minimizing the number of
irradiations needed and possible systematic errors in the
irradiation and in the subsequent activity determination.
Several actions have been implemented successfully in
order to validate the results obtained with this new technique: i) a PE foil has been placed in front of each multifoil stack (agreement within 1.8% between diﬀerent irradiation and beam energies), ii) the IAEA monitor reaction
cross section of nat Cu(p,x)63 Zn was measured (3% agreement with the evaluated cross section), iii) the beam energies were chosen such that some data points are measured
twice (agreement within uncertainties).
The multi-foil targets could not be employed to study
very low (below 30 MeV) energies. Therefore, a set of
single irradiations and production yield measurement with
conventional detectors, instead of the PET scanner, has
been performed, using again the evaluated nat Cu(p,x)63 Zn
production cross section for validation (agreement better
than 6%).
The outcome of this work is a total of seven excitation functions (40 energy points/reaction) from 30 up to
200 MeV. The production of 11 C, 13 N and 15 O in the
main elements of the human body and up to 200 MeV,
